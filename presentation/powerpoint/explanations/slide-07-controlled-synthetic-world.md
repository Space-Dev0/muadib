# Slide 7 — A controlled synthetic world

This guide explains what the seventh slide means, how the scenes and rules work, and why the exposure dataset and diffusion-training datasets must be described separately. The final section is a short script for the audience.

## 1. What is this slide trying to communicate?

We use small synthetic scenes so that we can control what is present and record exactly what the renderer placed. This lets us ask precise questions about colors, shapes, combinations and spatial relations. The slide shows a clean illustrative scene and reports two different bodies of work:

- An **exposure-measurement run** with 57 conditions and 5.7 million accepted synthetic scenes in total.
- A **separate diffusion pilot** with four datasets of 200 fixed training scenes each.

These are not the same dataset, and the larger number is not the number of training images used by each pilot model.

## 2. What is a synthetic scene?

A synthetic scene is an image generated by software from a scene description rather than captured by a camera. In the prototype renderer, images are 64 × 64 pixels, and the vocabulary includes red, blue, green and yellow; circles, squares and triangles are available shapes. Each object has a color, a shape and an `(x, y)` center.

The exposure-measurement renderer's base scene samples three objects on a 3 × 3 grid with small coordinate jitter. It draws at a higher resolution and resizes to 64 × 64. The separate pilot uses its own fixed two-object or three-object datasets. Do not assume that every dataset or experiment uses the identical base scene sampler.

A scene description might contain:

| Object | Color | Shape | Center `(x, y)` |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | red | circle | (15, 30) |
| B | blue | square | (45, 35) |
| C | green | triangle | (33, 52) |

These entries are an invented teaching example, not a claim about a particular displayed image. Coordinates record object centers. `x` increases to the right, while `y` increases downward in image coordinates.

The renderer can produce both the pixels and a record of the objects it placed. That record is useful for checking whether a generated **training scene description** satisfies a rule. It does not automatically tell us which objects are present in a new, noisy image generated by a diffusion model.

## 3. What are colors, shapes and predicates?

A **predicate** is a statement that can be true or false for a scene or for a chosen object assignment:

- `color(A) = red`
- `shape(A) = circle`
- `shape(B) = square`
- `left_of(A, B)`

A **rule** is a conjunction: the required predicates must all hold with consistent bindings. Consider the slide's example:

> “A red object is left of a distinct square.”

In the prototype semantics, this asks whether there is some assignment of object A and a different object B such that A is red, B is a square, and A's center is strictly left of B's center. The square may have the same color as the red object; **distinct means two different objects**, not necessarily different colors.

### What exactly does “left of” mean?

For the prototype rule checker, `left_of(A, B)` means

\[
 x_A < x_B.
\]

It is a strict comparison of object-center x-coordinates. It does **not** require A to be directly horizontal to B, to be completely to the left with no horizontal overlap, or to lie within a particular angular sector. Equal x-coordinates do not satisfy the strict relation. Other versions of a benchmark could choose stricter geometric definitions, but those would be different rules and must be specified.

For the teaching table above, `15 < 45`, so A is left of B. If A were at `(15, 52)` and B at `(45, 12)`, the same x-coordinate test would still say A is left of B even though their vertical positions differ substantially.

### Why does binding matter?

A scene containing a red square and a blue circle contains the individual events “red exists” and “circle exists.” Yet it contains no red circle. Correctly evaluating the bound rule requires one object that is both red and a circle; the color of one object cannot be combined with the shape of another.

The rule engine searches for an assignment of distinct objects to the rule's distinct variables. All predicates referring to the same variable must hold of the same assigned object. This is central to distinguishing isolated concept exposure from exposure to a complete combination.

## 4. Why build a controlled synthetic world?

In a large collection of photographs, many things vary at once: lighting, backgrounds, object types, camera angle, number of objects and co-occurrence frequencies. Those data are valuable, but it can be hard to identify which variation explains a model's performance.

Synthetic data give us tools to:

1. Choose a vocabulary of objects, attributes and relations.
2. Generate examples under specified conditions and seeds.
3. Keep a structured record of what was generated.
4. Estimate how often predicates and complete rules occur.
5. Design controlled changes to the distribution for future experiments.

Control is a research advantage, not a guarantee that the resulting study is unbiased. Generated scenes are simpler than natural images, and results on them do not automatically transfer to photographs or large pretrained text-to-image models.

## 5. How are conditioned scenes generated?

For the prototype measurement renderer, a rule can be supplied as a condition. Conceptually, the process is:

1. Sample a candidate scene from the base scene distribution.
2. Evaluate whether an assignment of objects satisfies the requested rule.
3. Accept the scene if the rule holds; otherwise draw another candidate.
4. Stop with an error if the configured maximum number of attempts is reached.

This is **rejection sampling**. A hard or rare condition can require many candidate draws per accepted scene. Failure within a finite attempt budget does not by itself prove a rule is impossible.

The accepted scenes are samples from the renderer conditional on the selected rule and sampling procedure. They are not automatically a representative sample of real-world images.

## 6. What do “57 conditions” and “5.7 million scenes” mean?

The documented exposure run used 57 candidate rule conditions. For each condition it collected 100,000 accepted scene descriptions:

\[
57\times100{,}000=5{,}700{,}000\text{ accepted scenes}.
\]

Approximately 57.8 million candidate attempts were needed in that run because scenes not matching their condition were rejected. The accepted count tells us the scale of the **renderer measurement**; it is not the size of a human-reviewed set or the number of outputs generated by a trained diffusion model.

For each candidate condition, the measurement pipeline estimates how often various events hold in its accepted scenes. These estimates form an exposure matrix. An illustrative entry of 0.30 in row `p`, column `j` would mean approximately 30% of the measured scenes from condition `j` satisfied event `p`. The actual row definitions, sampling and uncertainty matter.

## 7. What is the separate four-dataset diffusion pilot?

The pilot crosses two factors:

| Pilot factor | Choices |
|---|---|
| Scene complexity | 2 objects or 3 objects |
| Target-color balance | 50:50 or 90:10 |

The combination yields four conditions, with **200 fixed training scenes per condition**. A separate model was trained for each condition in the reported first block. Slide 8 explains the 500,000 optimizer updates per model and the evaluation sampling.

The pilot's target circles are red or blue, while distractors use different attributes. This means target role is confounded with color and shape. It is useful as a feasibility pilot but is not a direct test of novel attribute binding or the proposed joint-exposure intervention.

## 8. How does this connect to marginal and joint exposure?

Suppose event A is “a red object exists” and event B is “a circle exists.” Their **marginal exposures** tell us how often each exists in the training scene distribution, considered separately. The **bound-rule exposure** asks how often an object is both red and a circle.

In a one-object teaching example, joint exposure can be represented by P(A, B), as on slide 4. In a multi-object scene, however, “A exists somewhere and B exists somewhere” can be satisfied by different objects. That event must not be mistaken for the same-object red-circle rule.

The renderer's structured annotations let us distinguish those events when measuring synthetic scenes. The future study aims to change the complete-rule exposure while controlling relevant marginals, but this requires feasible mixture designs and careful validation.

## 9. Strengths and limits of the renderer

**Strengths:** explicit object metadata; repeatable scene generation; interpretable predicates; control of attributes, counts and relations; and the ability to measure candidate training distributions before model training.

**Limits:** a 64 × 64 flat-shape world is simplified; rejection can be inefficient; and some existential relation predicates become almost always true when any pair of several objects can satisfy them. A frequently true relation is a weak test of whether a model understands a demanding spatial relation. Template balance and binding structure also need careful checking.

These limits do not make synthetic work useless. They tell us how to interpret the current benchmark and what to improve before making stronger claims.

## 10. Likely evaluator questions

**Why 64 × 64?** It keeps the pilot computationally manageable and makes controlled training possible on limited hardware. It also limits detail and realism.

**Are 5.7 million scenes the pilot training set?** No. That is the accepted exposure-measurement total across 57 conditions. The separate pilot uses four fixed datasets of 200 scenes each.

**Does “distinct square” mean a different color?** No. It means a different object from the red object.

**Does “left of” mean directly to the left?** Not in the prototype rule described here. It is a strict center-x comparison; vertical alignment is not required.

**Are annotations automatically available for diffusion outputs?** No. The renderer provides metadata for its own scenes. Model-generated images need a validated checker and/or human evaluation.

## What to tell the audience

> “We built a controlled 64-by-64 world of colored shapes with known object attributes and positions. That lets us define rules such as a red object being left of a distinct square and measure whether complete combinations actually occur. Our large exposure measurement contains 5.7 million accepted synthetic scenes across 57 conditions. The separate model-feasibility pilot uses four much smaller datasets of 200 training scenes each; the two numbers answer different questions.”
