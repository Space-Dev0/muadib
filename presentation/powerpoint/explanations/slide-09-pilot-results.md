# Slide 9 — Pilot results: 64 reviewed samples

This guide explains the ninth slide, its bar chart, the four evaluation cells and the arithmetic behind the reported contrast ranges. The final section is a short script for the audience. The numbers refer to the **first fixed pilot-evaluation block**, not the separate 5.7-million-scene exposure measurement.

## 1. What is the slide trying to show?

The slide summarizes a small, prespecified review of images generated by four conditional diffusion models. The models correspond to a 2 × 2 pilot design:

- Training scenes contain either **two** or **three** objects.
- Requested target colors are **balanced 50:50** or **imbalanced 90:10** in the corresponding training dataset.

The primary review question is whether the generated image contains the **requested-color circle**. The slide shows the recorded yes/no/unresolved labels and two descriptive contrasts calculated from the four cells.

This is a feasibility pilot about object count and target-color balance. It is **not** a controlled test of whether changing joint exposure while fixing marginal exposure affects model performance.

## 2. How were the 64 generated images obtained?

In the first reported model-training block, one model was trained for each of the four conditions. Each model had 200 fixed training scenes and was trained for 500,000 optimizer updates with batch size 4. The fixed evaluation used EMA weights, 1,000 reverse sampling steps, guidance 1, two requested colors, and eight paired noise seeds.

Per condition:

\[
 2\;\text{requested colors}\times8\;\text{noise seeds}
 =16\;\text{generated images}.
\]

Across four conditions:

\[
 4\times16=64\;\text{generated images}.
\]

The target color is supplied as the model's generation condition. Expected object count was considered as a secondary review question; it was not an additional generation prompt in the primary target-circle evaluation.

A **noise seed** controls the randomness used to generate a sample from an already trained model. A **training seed** influences model initialization and training randomness. Eight noise seeds from one model are not eight independently trained models.

## 3. Read the chart and the exact counts

The bars summarize these recorded target-circle judgments:

| Training condition | Yes | No | Unresolved | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Low object count, balanced | 16 | 0 | 0 | 16 |
| Low object count, imbalanced | 15 | 0 | 1 | 16 |
| High object count, balanced | 14 | 2 | 0 | 16 |
| High object count, imbalanced | 14 | 1 | 1 | 16 |
| **Total** | **59** | **3** | **2** | **64** |

“Low” and “high” refer to two-object versus three-object **training conditions** in this pilot. Each bar represents 16 generated images. The segment labels show recorded yes, no and unresolved judgments; an unresolved label is retained rather than silently converted to no.

Of these 64 images, `59/64` were labeled yes, which is about 92.19% of this specific fixed set. This descriptive proportion is not, by itself, an estimate of performance on arbitrary prompts or a guarantee of composition.

The four returned review files for this first block were byte-identical. The files alone cannot independently establish who reviewed each image or whether reviews were independent. Identical files do not by themselves prove what the reviewers did. The team separately confirmed independent review for the later second block, whose artifacts likewise cannot independently verify reviewer identities.

## 4. What does an unresolved label mean?

An unresolved label means that the available review does not produce a defined yes/no answer under the scoring policy. It might reflect an ambiguous output or an uncertain response. The source wording is preserved, and terms such as “maybe” and “not sure” are treated as unresolved for the reported analysis.

It is incorrect to say that the two unresolved samples were definitely failures. It is also incorrect to count them as definite successes without a prespecified adjudication or sensitivity calculation.

For the entire set, assigning both unresolved labels as no gives 59 yes outcomes; assigning both as yes gives 61. Thus the yes proportion for these same 64 images can range from `59/64` to `61/64` under those assignments. This range addresses only the unresolved labels. It is **not** a statistical confidence interval for a population success rate.

## 5. What is a contrast?

A **contrast** is a specified difference or weighted combination of measured quantities. In this pilot, it compares average target-circle success rates across groups of cells. The analysis gives equal weight to the two relevant cells in each group.

Let:

- `s_LB`: success rate for low-object-count, balanced training.
- `s_LI`: success rate for low-object-count, imbalanced training.
- `s_HB`: success rate for high-object-count, balanced training.
- `s_HI`: success rate for high-object-count, imbalanced training.

Each cell has 16 generated images, so a cell success rate is its number of yes outcomes after a hypothetical resolution of uncertainty, divided by 16.

The displayed study defines a **complexity drop** and an **imbalance drop**:

\[
 D_C=\frac{s_{LB}+s_{LI}-s_{HB}-s_{HI}}{2},
\]

\[
 D_I=\frac{s_{LB}+s_{HB}-s_{LI}-s_{HI}}{2}.
\]

`D_C` compares the average of the two low-object-count conditions against the average of the two high-object-count conditions. `D_I` compares the average of the two balanced conditions against the average of the two imbalanced conditions. The `/2` averages the two cells on each side.

A positive drop means the first group in the stated subtraction has the higher success rate in these samples. A negative value means the reverse. The terms “complexity” and “imbalance” label the intended factors; they do not prove that all other differences between the training datasets were eliminated.

## 6. Work through all four assignments of the two unresolved labels

The unresolved images are in the **low/imbalanced** and **high/imbalanced** cells. Let `l` be the hypothetical success assignment of the low/imbalanced unresolved image and `h` that of the high/imbalanced unresolved image. Each is either 0 or 1.

The four cell success counts after assignment are:

\[
 LB=16,\quad LI=15+l,\quad HB=14,\quad HI=14+h.
\]

Because all cells contain 16 images,

\[
 D_C=\frac{16+(15+l)-14-(14+h)}{32}
     =\frac{3+l-h}{32},
\]

\[
 D_I=\frac{16+14-(15+l)-(14+h)}{32}
     =\frac{1-l-h}{32}.
\]

Now evaluate all four possibilities:

| `l` | `h` | Complexity drop `D_C` | Imbalance drop `D_I` |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 3/32 = 9.375 percentage points | 1/32 = 3.125 percentage points |
| 0 | 1 | 2/32 = 6.25 percentage points | 0 percentage points |
| 1 | 0 | 4/32 = 12.5 percentage points | 0 percentage points |
| 1 | 1 | 3/32 = 9.375 percentage points | −1/32 = −3.125 percentage points |

Therefore the reported ranges are:

\[
 D_C\in[0.0625,\,0.125],\qquad
 D_I\in[-0.03125,\,0.03125].
\]

The first interval is **6.25 to 12.5 percentage points**. The second is **−3.125 to 3.125 percentage points**. A percentage point is an absolute difference between percentages: for example, 90% minus 80% is 10 percentage points, not a 10% relative change.

These ranges are marginal summaries of the same assignments: not every combination of values from the two ranges can necessarily occur together. The table above shows the four joint possibilities.

## 7. Why are these bounds not confidence intervals?

The bounds answer a narrow question:

> “For these same 64 reviewed images, how much could the contrast change if each unresolved label were assigned either yes or no?”

They do not account for uncertainty from drawing different training datasets, training different models, generating new images, choosing a different evaluation set or using different reviewers. A **confidence interval** is constructed using a statistical procedure with a specified sampling interpretation; these worst-case assignment bounds are not such an interval.

The complexity-drop range is positive for all four assignments in this one block. The imbalance range includes zero and both signs. Those observations are descriptive for the fixed samples; they do not establish a population effect or the relative importance of the two factors across independent model and dataset runs.

The later documented second block produced 58 yes and 6 no labels and a descriptive complexity drop of zero. It used another training-seed block but shared the underlying dataset realization. That is useful context, but two small blocks with shared data do not establish a general replication or a completed research gate.

## 8. What else must we disclose about the pilot design?

- Each condition's training dataset contains only 200 fixed scenes, and repeated exposure to them raises a memorization question.
- Target circles are red or blue, while distractors have other attributes, so target role, shape and color are confounded.
- The four datasets were sampled separately rather than constructed as exact matched-geometry scene pairs.
- The requested output is a target-color circle, not a full held-out relational composition.
- The returned first-block review files were identical, so their contents alone do not establish independent reviewer provenance.

These points explain the practical limits of the interpretation. They do not erase the fact that the models were trained, samples were generated and a defined review was performed.

## 9. How does the automated checker relate to the graph?

The bar chart shows the **recorded human review labels**, not an automated checker's classification of every image. The experimental checker answered 46 of 64 and abstained on 18. Its 46 answers agreed with the recorded labels, but all were positive; it abstained on all three human-negative cases and both unresolved cases.

Therefore, agreement on the answered subset does not establish the checker's false-accept rate on negatives or its accuracy on all 64 samples. Checker coverage and abstentions must accompany agreement. Slide 10 discusses the selected images and checker behavior in more detail.

## 10. Does this slide answer “Which Exposure Matters?”

Not yet. Marginal exposure concerns the frequency of individual predicates in training; joint exposure concerns a specified full combination. The two factors on this slide are training-scene object count and target-color balance. They do not constitute the planned intervention that deliberately varies full-rule joint exposure while controlling relevant marginals.

The direct study still requires validated rules, feasible and measured exposure mixtures, independent dataset/model replications and a reliable way to evaluate complete generated rules. The current results provide feasibility and evaluation diagnostics for preparing that study.

## 11. Likely evaluator questions

**Why are there 16 images per cell?** The fixed protocol generated two requested colors for each of eight paired noise seeds.

**Does 59/64 mean 92% generalization?** It is a yes-label proportion for this fixed target-circle sample. It does not establish generalization to held-out compositions, other datasets or a population of independently trained models.

**Why does the complexity range have a denominator of 32?** Each cell has 16 images, and the contrast averages two cell rates per side, introducing another factor of two: `16 * 2 = 32`.

**Why is the imbalance range centered on zero?** With the allowed assignments, its numerator is `1 - l - h`, which takes values 1, 0 and −1.

**Are the two ranges confidence intervals?** No. They are exact sensitivity bounds for the two unresolved labels in this fixed set of images.

**Does the checker have 100% accuracy?** It agreed on the 46 cases it answered, all positive. It abstained on all three human-negative cases, so overall accuracy and false-accept behavior are not established by that agreement.

## What to tell the audience

> “We generated 16 images for each of four pilot conditions, giving 64 reviewed outputs. The recorded target-circle judgments were 59 yes, three no and two unresolved. When we assign the unresolved labels both ways, the complexity contrast ranges from 6.25 to 12.5 percentage points and the imbalance contrast ranges from minus 3.125 to plus 3.125 points. These are bounds for ambiguity in this fixed image set, not confidence intervals. The pilot tests our training and evaluation workflow; it does not yet answer the joint-exposure hypothesis.”
